The first research component is skill extraction from employment-related text. This task asks a simple question: given a sentence or document, which words or spans actually refer to skills, tools, knowledge areas, or professional capabilities? In practice, this is a hard problem because hiring text is messy. Some skills are explicit, like “Python” or “TensorFlow.” Others are implicit, like “built scalable data pipelines,” which suggests skills in ETL, distributed systems, or data engineering even if those labels are not written directly. Some phrases are also ambiguous. “Communication” can be a real soft skill requirement, but “communicate with stakeholders” may not always need to be tagged as a skill mention depending on annotation rules. SkillSpan formalized this problem as span-level extraction from job postings and introduced the SkillSpan dataset for hard and soft skill extraction. 

In your thesis, this component matters because the whole recommendation system depends on the quality of extracted information. If the extractor misses important skills, the matcher will score good jobs too low. If the extractor pulls out noisy or irrelevant phrases, the matcher will score bad jobs too high. So the extraction stage is not just preprocessing. It directly controls recommendation quality. A weak extractor makes the whole system shaky. A strong extractor makes the rest of the pipeline smarter, more accurate, and easier to explain.

There are a few important research directions inside this component. One direction is better span detection, meaning the model becomes more accurate at finding the boundaries of skill mentions. Another is generalization, meaning the model works well not only on one dataset but also on new resumes, new job descriptions, and rare skill patterns. NNOSE is important here because it proposes a retrieval-augmented approach: instead of relying only on the encoder’s internal knowledge, it retrieves similar examples from an external datastore and uses them to improve extraction. The EACL 2024 paper reports substantial gains, especially for infrequent patterns and cross-dataset settings, with up to 30 percent span-F1 improvement in some transfer scenarios.

Another direction is LLM-based skill extraction. Skill-LLM explores whether a fine-tuned large language model can outperform earlier task-specific approaches on skill extraction benchmarks. Its paper reports that the proposed model outperforms earlier state-of-the-art techniques on the benchmark used in the study. Whether you choose to use it or not, it matters in the literature review because it represents the current push toward larger, instruction-capable models for extraction.

For your thesis, the first research component can be framed as: How can skills be extracted from resumes and job postings accurately enough to support reliable recommendation and explanation? That gives you several concrete sub-problems to study: handling rare skills, handling paraphrased skill mentions, improving generalization from job postings to resumes, and normalizing different surface forms of the same skill. The output of this component is not a recommendation yet. It is a structured skill representation that the second component can use.

A good final statement for this component is: Skill extraction converts unstructured resume and job text into machine-usable evidence about what a candidate knows and what a job requires.
